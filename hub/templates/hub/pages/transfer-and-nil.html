    Read this first

    What this guide is, and what it isn't

    This is a plain-language summary, written for families, of rules published by the associations themselves. Every factual claim below is numbered and linked to its source at the foot of the page.

    It is not legal advice and it is not a substitute for the rulebooks. Eligibility decisions are made by your school's athletic director, your local school system, and the association — not by this page. Rules change, sometimes mid-year. Before making any decision that affects your athlete's eligibility, confirm it with your AD in writing and read the source document linked here.

    The most common expensive mistake

    Families transfer first and ask about eligibility afterward. In several of the jurisdictions below, the consequence of a transfer is fixed the moment the student enrolls, and no appeal undoes it. Ask before you enroll, not after.

    Which rulebook applies to you

    Four associations, one metro area

    Where your athlete plays determines which rules govern them — and families here routinely cross these lines, living in one jurisdiction, playing club ball in another, and attending school in a third.

    • MPSSAA — Maryland public secondary schools. Plus your county's own rules, which can be stricter.
    • MIAA (boys) and IAAM (girls) — Maryland private and independent schools.
    • DCSAA — District of Columbia schools.
    • VHSL — Virginia public schools.
    • VISAA — Virginia independent schools.

    Moving between two schools inside the same association is one situation. Moving between associations — a Maryland public school to a DC private school, say — is a different one, and it's the case families most often get wrong.

    Rules stack — this is the thing to understand

    An athlete is almost never governed by one rulebook. There are three layers, and the strictest one wins:

    1. The state association (MPSSAA, DCSAA, VHSL) sets the floor. 2. The league or conference he actually plays in — DCIAA, the WCAC — can be stricter. 3. The local school system — his Maryland county — can be stricter again.

    Checking only the state association is the most common way families get a confident, wrong answer. Both examples below prove it: DCSAA allows immediate eligibility in some cases while DCIAA imposes a full year, and Montgomery County imposes a year where MPSSAA alone might not.

    Private-school NIL is a blank across the whole region

    The NIL rows above cover the public associations. Nothing in the MPSSAA guidance governs a Maryland private school, and nothing in VHSL's governs a Virginia independent school.

    Both Maryland private-school handbooks have now been read directly, and they agree: neither the MIAA nor the IAAM has any NIL policy. Each instead carries a broadly worded amateurism rule barring payments, gratuities and gifts arising from sports participation, and each requires athletes to follow NCAA standards for avoiding professionalism.14,32

    That is a different legal position from the public associations, not a softer one. A Maryland public-school athlete has written permission to do NIL deals within stated limits. A Maryland private-school athlete has no stated permission and an explicit prohibition on receiving payment. For VISAA and the WCAC, nothing is verified in either direction.

    A private-school athlete doing an NIL deal on the assumption that public-school rules apply is guessing — and the guess runs against the only written rule that does apply to them. Ask the league office in writing before signing anything.

    Maryland public · MPSSAA

    Your county rules can be stricter than the state's

    This is the single most overlooked fact for Maryland families, and it catches people every year. MPSSAA's own guidance directs schools and parents to consult their Local School System on eligibility questions, because an LSS may be more restrictive than MPSSAA regulations.3

    In practice that means Montgomery County, Prince George's County, Howard County, Baltimore County and the rest each maintain their own athletic transfer procedures on top of the state rules. Prince George's County publishes a dedicated administrative procedure for athletic transfers.9 Reading only the MPSSAA rules will give you an incomplete — and possibly wrong — answer.

    Montgomery County: one full calendar year

    MCPS is the clearest example of a county rule that bites harder than the state's. A student who transfers out of their current feeder pattern through a Change of School Assignment (COSA) must attend the new school for one calendar year before being eligible for MCPS interscholastic athletics, unless an athletic waiver is granted.12

    There are named exceptions. Students who change assignment within the Downcounty Consortium (DCC), the Northeast Consortium (NEC), or by administrative transfer do not need an athletic waiver and may participate at the new school.12

    Where exceptional circumstances exist, a parent or guardian may appeal by applying for an athletic waiver — but note the sequencing: waivers are not considered until the COSA itself has been approved.12 The Department of Systemwide Athletics handles these.

    Guidance — not a rule

    If you're in Montgomery County and considering a COSA for a rising senior, treat the one-year rule as the default outcome and the waiver as uncertain. A senior who transfers and is held out a calendar year has lost their final season — and with it, most of their recruiting window.

    Prince George's County: timing and paperwork decide it

    PGCPS is a county where basketball transfers are common, and its rules are structured very differently from Montgomery's. Administrative Procedure 5110.4, effective 1 July 2024, governs athletic transfers.9 Rather than a fixed sit-out period, eligibility here turns on when you move and whether the paperwork clears.

    • In-county, out of season. A student transferring before the first competition date may participate once the paperwork is approved.9
    • In-county, during the season. The student becomes "immediately ineligible for the remainder of the in-season sport played at the previous school" and may not play that same sport at the new school for that season.9 Other sports aren't necessarily affected — the restriction is sport-specific.
    • Out-of-county. The student stays ineligible until documentation is approved, and is "ineligible to start the season or join a team once the regional and state playoffs for the season have begun."9

    The PGCPS paperwork, in full

    Because approval — not a waiting period — is what gates eligibility here, the documentation is the process. AP 5110.4 requires two proof-of-residency forms plus:9

    • Athletic Transfer Screening Form
    • Registration form showing the legal address
    • Official transcript and report card
    • County-approved transfer documentation
    • Supporting documents — deed, utility bill, lease, proof of guardianship, and similar

    PGCPS also codifies the participation ceiling: a maximum of four seasons, with no participation "beyond their fourth year of high school, beginning from their 9th grade COHORT year." A student cannot repeat twelfth grade and continue playing.9

    The appeal clock is short — 10 business days

    If a student is ruled ineligible, parents have 10 business days from the date of the official letter to appeal to the Office of Appeals, and a hearing committee convenes within 10 business days to review the case.9 That is a fast clock. Open the letter the day it arrives and diary the deadline immediately — an appeal not filed in time is simply over.

    Guidance — not a rule

    Two counties, two completely different strategies. In Montgomery, the question is whether you can survive a calendar year out, so the decision is about timing relative to his remaining seasons. In Prince George's, the question is whether the documents are complete and approved before the first competition date, so the decision is about moving early and having the paperwork perfect. Same state, same association, opposite playbooks.

    Howard and Anne Arundel

    Both counties gate school assignment on bona fide residence first, with a formal process for attending a school outside your assigned area — and that process is where athletic questions get decided.

    • Howard County (HCPSS). Students attend the school for their attendance area based on bona fide residence, under Policy 9000. Attending elsewhere requires Student Reassignment, which has four categories: Relocation; Build, Buy or Lease; Hardship; and Employee.17,18
    • Anne Arundel County (AACPS). A student living in the county who wants a school other than their boundary school files an out-of-area transfer request, governed by regulation JAB-RA. AACPS also publishes an annual Guide for Student Athletes and Parents, which is the right document for eligibility questions.19,20

    Verified partially — ask before relying on this

    For both counties this guide has confirmed the transfer and reassignment processes but has not confirmed what each one does to athletic eligibility specifically — whether there's a sit-out like Montgomery's, or a paperwork gate like Prince George's. Given how far apart those two turned out to be, do not assume either pattern carries over. Ask the county athletics office directly.

    Charles County: a calendar year, with one narrow exception

    Charles County is among the strictest verified here, and the rule is stated plainly: high school students who transfer from out of zone at any time during the school year are ineligible for interscholastic athletics at the new school for one calendar year from the date of transfer.27

    The timing detail matters, because the two cases produce different end dates:27

    • Transferred on or before the first day of school — ineligible for that one school year.
    • Transferred after the first day of school — ineligible for one full calendar year from the date of initial enrollment, which will run into the following school year.

    There is one stated exception: where the child of a CCPS employee is granted a transfer to a school in the zone of the parent or guardian's primary work location, that child remains eligible for athletics and extracurriculars provided they meet attendance and academic requirements.27

    Two further CCPS requirements: a new transfer student must provide a transcript or report card showing grades and attendance for the prior semester, and student-athletes must carry a minimum of four classes. CCPS also requires conformance with the MPSSAA Handbook on top of its own rules.27 The Board updated its eligibility policy in 2024, so use the current version rather than an older handbook.28

    Baltimore City and Baltimore County — two different systems

    These are separate school systems with separate rules, and they're easy to confuse. Baltimore City Public Schools has competed under MPSSAA since 1993 and administers athletics through its own interscholastic athletics office.29

    Baltimore City: not verified

    Despite being a significant basketball jurisdiction, City Schools' specific transfer-eligibility rule was not findable in published form during this research. What's confirmed is only that MPSSAA rules apply and that athletics are centrally administered.29 Call the City Schools athletics office directly — and treat any third-party summary of Baltimore City transfer rules with suspicion, including ones that look authoritative.

    Baltimore County (BCPS) does publish its rule, as Rule 6702. Two provisions stand out:30,31

    • A student entering a BCPS high school after 30 September and before 15 November is ineligible for the first semester — unless that late entrance is a transfer from a school the student had been attending regularly during the current school year.30
    • A student attending a BCPS high school without residing in that school's attendance area, and/or without written authority from the superintendent's designee or a pupil personnel worker, will be declared ineligible for interscholastic athletics.30,31

    Transfers at the state level

    MPSSAA ties ineligibility to enrollment timing, with the fourth Friday of the school year as the operative cutoff. For a student who just misses being enrolled at the new school by that fourth Friday, the period of ineligibility may be reduced — with Executive Committee approval — to 90 scheduled school days of enrollment, which does not count days when practice is held in August.3 A student who changes schools in the second half of the year (enrolled before the fourth Friday of February) becomes eligible on 1 August for fall sports.3

    The governing regulatory framework sits in COMAR 13A.06.03, Maryland's state regulations on interscholastic athletics, which also covers MPSSAA appeals procedure.3,13 The full rule text and current-year dates belong in the MPSSAA Handbook — read it rather than planning from this summary.13

    Separately: students in grades 9 through 12 may compete for a maximum of four seasons in any one sport, and must maintain amateur status.3 That amateur-status requirement is what makes the NIL rules below matter — NIL is the carve-out that lets an athlete earn without forfeiting it.

    NIL

    Maryland public school athletes may engage in NIL activity and keep their eligibility, following guidance the MPSSAA adopted after a December Board of Control meeting; Maryland was reported as the 24th state to permit it.2 The core restriction is a clean separation between the athlete and the school: no reference to a member school or the MPSSAA, and no team jerseys, school name, mascot, logo or other identifying marks when marketing NIL products or services.1,2 Uniquely among state associations at the time, the guidance also prohibits NIL deals promoting video games.2

    Maryland private · MIAA

    What we can confirm, and what we pulled

    Correction — 19 September 2026

    An earlier version of this page described MIAA transfer rules — including a "Form 200" — sourced from miaa.net. That domain belongs to the Massachusetts Interscholastic Athletic Association, a different organisation that shares the same initials. Those rules were Massachusetts rules and have been removed.

    The Maryland MIAA's official site is marylandiaa.prestosports.com.8 If you read an earlier version of this page, disregard anything it said about MIAA transfers.

    Verified

    The MIAA is a boys' sports conference for private high schools, centred on the Baltimore metropolitan area but extending across the state including the Eastern Shore. It has 27 member schools competing in 17 sports, and played its first official schedule in the autumn of 1995, formed after Baltimore City schools left the Maryland Scholastic Association.8

    On eligibility, one procedural point is confirmed by MIAA eligibility verification forms published by member schools: a transfer student may not participate until an eligibility verification form is submitted to the league office and approval is granted in writing.10 Approval is therefore the gate — not the passage of time alone.

    Transfers — Article 6

    Now sourced from the Maryland MIAA's own handbook.32

    • Mid-year transfer. A student who transfers mid-year and had participated at any level is ineligible "for the remainder of that school year."32
    • The 30-day rule. Transfers between 1 September and 30 May must wait 30 days before competing in league contests.32
    • Former varsity athletes moving between MIAA schools. This is the provision to read twice: a former varsity athlete may participate only in "non-conference games" for one calendar year from enrollment. Undersquad athletes may transfer without penalty.32
    • Open enrollment, 1 June to 1 September. A transfer from a non-member school to a member school carries no penalty if enrollment documentation is complete by 1 September.32
    • Program elimination. Restrictions are waived where a school eliminates the athlete's sport.32
    • All transfers require an Eligibility Verification Form submitted before competition.10,32

    Note how the varsity provision differs from a plain sit-out: the athlete isn't barred from playing, but is confined to non-conference games for a year. For a recruit, that means a season largely outside the conference schedule — which is a different kind of cost than sitting entirely, and worth understanding precisely.

    MIAA has no NIL policy either

    Amateur status sits at Article 4, Section 4: a student must be an amateur and "may not receive, directly or indirectly, any salary, stipend, incentive, payment, award, gratuity, gift" resulting from sports participation. Athletes are required to "adhere to the policies for avoiding 'professionalism' as defined by the NCAA."32

    There are narrow carve-outs for elite sport camps, where "travel, accommodations and meals are considered acceptable gratuities" — but items of intrinsic value such as helmets or equipment bags are prohibited.32

    The handbook contains no explicit NIL language.32 That mirrors the IAAM exactly.

    Guidance — not a rule

    Get MIAA rules from marylandiaa.prestosports.com or the handbook at miaahandbook.com, and confirm any document you're handed says Maryland on it. Checking the domain before trusting the content is the single easiest mistake to avoid in Maryland private-school athletics.

    Girls' schools: the IAAM

    The Interscholastic Athletic Association of Maryland is a separate association from the MIAA, covering 31 member schools. It publishes a current handbook, and the rules below come from the 2026–27 edition.14 Do not assume MIAA rules apply.

    Transfers — Article Two, Section 7

    The governing principle is stated directly in the handbook: "A student-athlete may not represent two schools in athletics in the same academic year regardless of the competitive level she has participated."14

    • Mid-year transfers. A student transferring during the academic year cannot participate in athletics at any level if she competed at her previous school — junior varsity participation does not exempt her.14
    • The open enrollment window. From 1 June through 1 September, a student-athlete may transfer without penalty, provided enrollment contracts are finalised by 1 September.14 This is the clean path, and it is a hard date.
    • Residency exception. A transfer is permitted without penalty where the family moves more than 30 miles from the primary residence.14
    • Program elimination exception. Permitted where the previous school eliminates the athlete's sport.14

    Eligibility — Article Two

    Full-time students in good standing are eligible upon enrollment. A student may not participate after her 19th birthday if that falls before 31 August; may compete a maximum of four seasons per sport across grades 9–12; may play two IAAM sports in a season with written notification; and is ineligible once she has graduated from secondary education.14

    IAAM has no NIL carve-out — and that's the finding

    Unlike MPSSAA, DCSAA and VHSL, the IAAM handbook contains no NIL policy. What it has instead is a broad amateurism rule at Article Two, Section 3: a student "must be an amateur…may not receive, directly or indirectly, any salary, stipend, incentive, payment, financial award, gratuity, gift, educational expenses or expense allowance as a result of participation in any sport."14

    It further states that "to preserve students' eligibility for college athletics, IAAM athletes are required to adhere to the policies for avoiding 'professionalism' as defined by the NCAA."14

    So where a Maryland public school athlete has an explicit, written permission to do NIL deals, an IAAM athlete has no stated permission and a broadly worded prohibition on receiving payment. This guide will not tell you what that means for a specific deal — that is a question for the league office, in writing, before anything is signed.

    An earlier version of this section said IAAM rules could not be verified. That was true of the sources then available; the current handbook has since been read directly, and the above replaces it.

    Virginia private · VISAA

    Two provisions seniors should know

    Virginia's independent schools are governed by the Virginia Independent Schools Athletic Association, which publishes an annual policy manual and a rules and guidelines document.15,16 Two provisions matter most for a transferring senior:

    • The senior-year transfer provision. A student who has been classified as a senior at any school and then transfers to another VISAA member school is eligible for VISAA events during that transfer year only, and may not gain additional eligibility afterward.15
    • The 30-day enrollment requirement. Students must be enrolled and in attendance at the member school at least 30 days before the start of the VISAA championship in that sport.15 A late-season move can therefore cost postseason eligibility even where regular-season play is fine.

    VISAA publishes its policy manual by academic year — the current edition is the 2026–2027 Policy Manual.15,16 Confirm against it rather than an archived year.

    VISAA NIL — not verified

    VISAA's rules page links to the policy manual but does not publish NIL or amateurism text on the page itself, and the manual is distributed as a document this guide could not read.16 No VISAA NIL or amateur-status policy is stated here in either direction. A Virginia independent-school athlete should not assume VHSL's NIL permissions apply — VHSL governs public schools.

    Washington DC · DCSAA

    The most permissive on transfers — with conditions

    DCSAA provides for immediate eligibility upon registration for a transferring student, provided they meet three conditions and all other DCSAA eligibility requirements:5

    • They have not previously participated in the sport they now want to compete in;
    • They are released by a proper school authority from the sending school;
    • They have completed registration at the receiving school.

    Note how much work that first condition does. A basketball player who has already played basketball at their previous school does not obviously fall inside this provision, and that is exactly the case most families are asking about. Read the full transfer requirements and confirm with the AD before assuming immediate eligibility.5

    DCSAA also prohibits member school representatives from engaging in any activity seeking to influence a student to transfer between member schools for athletic purposes.5

    But if he plays DCPS sports, DCIAA is the rule that binds

    This is the layering problem in its clearest form, and it's the single easiest way for a DC family to get a wrong answer.

    DCIAA: one full calendar year

    Under the DC Interscholastic Athletic Association — the league DCPS schools compete in — any student who transfers schools after beginning 9th grade is ineligible for one calendar year (365 days), with the clock starting on the first day of enrollment at the new school. Waivers are available in limited circumstances.21,22

    Read that against the DCSAA provision above. The state association describes conditions for immediate eligibility; the league a DCPS athlete actually plays in imposes a year. Where they differ, the stricter one governs his season.

    DC's landscape has a further wrinkle worth knowing: alongside DCPS schools there are public charter schools and private schools, which may compete in different leagues under the DCSAA umbrella. Which league the receiving school plays in is therefore a question to ask explicitly, because it determines which transfer rule applies.21

    Crosses all three · WCAC

    The conference where much of DMV basketball is played

    The Washington Catholic Athletic Conference is a special case in this guide because it spans DC, Maryland and Virginia — its member schools sit in all three jurisdictions. A transfer between two WCAC schools can cross a state line without ever leaving the conference, and it's the conference rule that decides eligibility.

    Its transfer policy is unusually specific, and the senior-year provision is the one to read first:23,24

    • A 12th grade student is not permitted an intra-conference transfer. A senior who transfers to another WCAC member school is ineligible to play any varsity sports.23,24
    • 9th, 10th and 11th graders get one. Students in those grades are permitted a one-time intra-conference transfer, and must be enrolled at the new school on or before 15 September of that school year to be eligible for varsity that year.23,24
    • Using it after a varsity junior season carries a cost. A student who plays a varsity sport in 11th grade and then uses the one-time transfer is ineligible in that same sport for 365 days from enrollment at the new school.23,24
    • Junior varsity is unrestricted. The policy applies only to varsity participation; there are no transfer restrictions on JV.23,24

    Guidance — not a rule

    If a senior is weighing a move between WCAC schools for basketball reasons, the published policy points one way: it ends his varsity season. That is the clearest single-sentence answer anywhere in this guide, and it is worth confirming directly with both athletic directors before anything is signed. The conference publishes an annual athletics handbook — read the current year's edition, not a summary.24

    WCAC NIL — no policy found, but there is a workshop

    No WCAC NIL policy document was locatable in this research, and the conference handbook could not be read directly. Treat WCAC NIL as unverified.

    What did surface is more useful than a summary would have been: the conference has been running NIL education for student-athletes and families, including an NIL Educational Workshop in partnership with the law firm Offit Kurman, scheduled at The Catholic University of America in Washington DC for 1 October 2026.33

    Guidance — not a rule

    If your athlete is at a WCAC school, that workshop is the shortest path to an authoritative answer — a conference-run session with lawyers present beats any web search, including this page. Confirm the date and registration with the conference or your athletic director, since details can change.

    NIL

    DC athletes may participate in NIL without losing eligibility, so long as no school team, school, or DCSAA affiliation name or logo is visible in the advertisement. DCSAA additionally requires that it be notified of all commercial or marketing endorsements.4 That notification duty is a real obligation and appears to be stricter than the neighbouring jurisdictions — don't skip it. DCSAA publishes a dedicated NIL Policy Guide for student athletes.4

    Virginia public · VHSL

    The rule that changed on 1 July 2026

    This is the newest and least understood change in the metro. VHSL adopted a transfer bylaw — Transfer Rule Exception 28A-7-2(5) — that took effect 1 July 2026.6

    Under it, rising ninth graders must be coming from the middle school that feeds that particular high school in order to be eligible. A student from outside the zone must be approved into an academy or specialty program at the high school. Students who transfer into a school's feeder system without making a legitimate physical move into the attendance zone will be ineligible for athletics for their entire ninth-grade year — a full season sit-out, regardless of sport.6

    Because the rule took effect on 1 July 2026, reporting at the time noted that the then-current eighth grade cohort was not affected.6 If you are planning a move involving a rising freshman in Virginia, this is the provision to read in full and discuss with the school before enrolling.

    Northern Virginia: ineligible is the default

    Fairfax County is the largest school system in the region and the clearest statement of how Virginia works. Per FCPS: by default, all students who change from one school to another are ineligible for VHSL athletic participation at the new school unless they satisfy the criteria of VHSL Rule 28A-7-1, the transfer rule.25

    Note how that inverts Prince George's County. In PG, a properly timed and documented transfer can clear with no sit-out. In Fairfax, ineligibility is the starting position and the family has to earn their way out of it.

    To begin, students complete the FCPS Guardian Transfer Eligibility Application. The bar for a qualifying move is high: the entire family must make the change and take household goods with them, the change must be made with the intent that it is permanent, and both the sending and receiving school principals must complete and file the VHSL Transfer Form.25

    Two recent changes matter. In early 2025 FCPS worked with VHSL to revamp statewide transfer and eligibility policy, including prohibitions on transferring purely for athletic reasons and a rule making athletes who complete the regular season and then transfer ineligible for postseason play.25,26 And FCPS now centrally manages every transfer across all its high schools rather than leaving it to individual schools.25

    Guidance — not a rule

    Prince William, Loudoun, Arlington and Alexandria all operate under the same VHSL rules, but each runs its own transfer process and paperwork. This guide has verified Fairfax's specifically. Treat VHSL Rule 28A-7-1 as the common floor and ask your own division's athletics office how they administer it.

    NIL

    VHSL has permitted NIL since 1 July 2023.7 Two things define the policy. First, pay-for-play is prohibited, and the board has been explicit that agreements must reflect the value of a specific service or activity an athlete provides — not an incentive tied to enrollment decisions or team membership.7 Second, deals with corporate partners in these industries are strictly prohibited: adult entertainment, alcohol, tobacco and e-smoking, opioids, casinos, gambling, sports betting, weapons, firearms and ammunition.7 The Virginia Department of Education also maintains an NIL resource page.11

    Before you act

    What to ask, and who to ask

    Guidance — not a rule

    • Ask before enrolling. Put the question to the receiving school's athletic director in writing, and keep the reply. Verbal assurances don't survive a dispute.
    • Ask the sending school too. Release from the prior school is a condition in at least one jurisdiction here, and a missing release can be the whole problem.
    • In Maryland, ask your county, not just the state. The local school system's rules sit on top of MPSSAA's and can be stricter.
    • Get the paperwork early. MIAA families should ask for the eligibility verification form at the first conversation, not the last — participation requires written approval from the league office.
    • Check the domain on anything you read. Several associations in different states share initials. Confirm a document says Maryland, Virginia or District of Columbia before acting on it.
    • For NIL, notify where required and keep the school out of the frame. No jerseys, no logos, no school name — in every jurisdiction verified here.
    • If money is involved, talk to a professional. NIL income for a minor raises tax and contract questions this guide does not address.

    Honesty

    What this guide does not yet cover

    Listing the gaps is part of the standard. These are open and will be closed in the next revision:

    • IAAM — now closed. The 2026–27 handbook has been read directly; transfers, eligibility and amateur status are in the IAAM section above.
    • VISAA NIL and amateur status — the 2026–27 policy manual is published as a document this guide could not read. Transfers are verified; NIL is not.
    • WCAC NIL policy — no policy document located. Its transfer policy is verified; whether it has a written NIL policy, and how that interacts with three different state associations, is not.
    • NIL notification duties outside DC — DCSAA clearly requires notification. Whether MPSSAA, MIAA or VHSL require disclosure is unconfirmed.
    • Howard and Anne Arundel athletic consequences — the reassignment and out-of-area transfer processes are verified; what each does to athletic eligibility is not.
    • Baltimore City's transfer rule — not findable in published form. Confirmed only that MPSSAA governs and athletics are centrally administered. This gap needs a phone call, not a search.
    • Prince William, Loudoun, Arlington and Alexandria — same VHSL floor as Fairfax, but each administers transfers its own way. Only Fairfax is verified here.
    • The current DCIAA handbook — the 365-day rule is sourced to DCIAA's own transfers page, but the handbook copy found was a 2019 edition. Request the current one from DCPS Athletics.
    • PGCPS bona fide residence-change exceptions — the procedure's residency requirements are documented here, but whether a genuine family move creates an explicit exception, and on what terms, was not confirmed. Ask the county directly.
    • The MPSSAA NIL guidance PDF — cited via reporting and the document's location; the full text should be read directly and this page updated with anything the summaries omit.
    • VHSL bylaw text — the 1 July 2026 transfer rule is sourced to reporting. It should be confirmed against the VHSL handbook itself before this page is relied on.

    If you read one thing twice

    The rules differ by jurisdiction, they differ by public versus private, in Maryland they differ by county, and Virginia's changed in July. There is no single answer to "does my athlete sit out" — only the answer for your specific move, from your AD, in writing, before you enroll.

    The same move, made the same week, across four jurisdictions an hour apart:

    • Prince George's County — before the first competition date with complete paperwork, potentially no sit-out at all.
    • Montgomery County — out of the feeder pattern by COSA, a full calendar year unless waived.
    • Charles County — out of zone, a full calendar year from the transfer date, almost no exceptions.
    • Baltimore County — depends on the calendar date of entry and on residing in the attendance area.
    • DCPS / DCIAA — after 9th grade has begun, 365 days, waivers limited.
    • Fairfax County — ineligible by default, and the family must qualify under VHSL Rule 28A-7-1 to change that.
    • WCAC, as a senior — no varsity at all.

    That's the whole argument. There is no DMV answer to "does my athlete sit out" — only the answer for his specific school, league and county, from his AD, in writing, before he enrolls.
